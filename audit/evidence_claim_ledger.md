# Evidence and Source Audit

Audit date: August 27, 2026

The lecture is educational and does not provide patient-specific advice. The project source bank's AAP PREP export is the source of the ten question stems and answer keys. External sources were used to check the teaching explanations and guardrails. Plot values are synthetic unless a figure explicitly reproduces values in a question stem.

## Claim ledger

| Claim used in the deck | Claim type | Exact source | Supporting result or passage | Status | Contradictions and limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stable incidence with longer disease duration increases prevalence. | Epidemiologic definition | AAP PREP learner-mode export, Question 10; Mann CJ. *Emerg Med J*. 2003;20:54-60. PMID [12533370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12533370/), DOI [10.1136/emj.20.1.54](https://doi.org/10.1136/emj.20.1.54) | The PREP rationale distinguishes the inflow of new cases from the pool living with disease. Mann defines incidence and prevalence and explains their different time dimensions. | Verified | The approximation `prevalence ≈ incidence × duration` is most useful under roughly steady-state conditions; the deck states this limitation in speaker notes. |
| Repeatability without a reference standard assesses precision, not accuracy, sensitivity, or predictive value. | Measurement principle | PREP export, Question 24; FDA, [Statistical Guidance on Reporting Results from Studies Evaluating Diagnostic Tests](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/statistical-guidance-reporting-results-studies-evaluating-diagnostic-tests-guidance-industry-and-fda) | The PREP item keys precision. FDA guidance distinguishes agreement/repeatability analyses from comparisons against a reference standard. | Verified | Repeated measures alone cannot determine closeness to truth. |
| Measuring exposure and outcome during the same short interval is cross-sectional and generally leaves temporality unresolved. | Study-design definition | PREP export, Question 20; Mann CJ. PMID [12533370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12533370/) | Mann describes cross-sectional studies as observations at one point in time; the PREP vignette measures video-game use and BMI in the same month. | Verified | Cross-sectional studies can estimate prevalence and association, but causal direction is often ambiguous. |
| Random allocation provides the strongest listed design for estimating a treatment's causal effect. | Causal-design principle | PREP export, Question 22; Altman DG, Bland JM. *BMJ*. 1998;317:1301. DOI [10.1136/bmj.317.7168.1301](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.317.7168.1301); Nissen T, Wynn R. *BMC Res Notes*. 2014;7:264. PMID [24758689](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24758689/) | Randomization makes assignment independent of baseline prognosis in expectation and supplies a concurrent counterfactual comparison. | Verified | Randomization does not rescue poor allocation concealment, follow-up, adherence, outcome measurement, or analysis. |
| A case series is a reasonable descriptive first design for a rare disorder, but it cannot estimate incidence, relative risk, or a causal effect without a denominator or comparator. | Study-design principle | PREP export, Question 23; Nissen and Wynn. PMID [24758689](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24758689/) | The PREP item presents eight unusual HLH cases and keys a case series; the methodological review describes case series as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. | Verified | Selection, missing-record, and publication bias limit generalization. |
| A randomized diet-versus-statin trial can still be open-label when participants and the treating team know allocation. | Masking definition | PREP export, Question 25; Kahan BC et al. *Trials*. 2014;15:456. DOI [10.1186/1745-6215-15-456](https://doi.org/10.1186/1745-6215-15-456) | The intervention is visibly different across groups, so participant and treating-team masking is infeasible; randomization and masking are separate design features. | Verified with source caveat | The PREP critique incorrectly suggests that an active-comparator randomized study is “not controlled.” The deck does not repeat that wording. |
| A graded exposure-outcome relationship is a biological gradient that supports, but does not prove, causation. | Causal-reasoning principle | PREP export, Question 18; Hill AB. *Proc R Soc Med*. 1965;58:295-300. PMID [14283879](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14283879/), DOI [10.1177/003591576505800503](https://doi.org/10.1177/003591576505800503) | Hill discusses biological gradient as one consideration in causal reasoning and explicitly frames the considerations as viewpoints rather than mechanical proof. | Verified | The PREP vignette is a hypothetical teaching example; the deck explicitly states that it is not evidence that cellular towers cause autism. |
| The exact booth-age data are strongly right-skewed; the bubble-booth mean is 3.25 years and median is 2 years. | Recalculation from source data | PREP export, Question 15; values independently recomputed in `assets/generate_visuals.R` | The plotted ages retain all source counts. Direct calculation reproduces the stated mean and median. | Verified | The item teaches a conventional distributional assumption. In practice, Welch methods, transformations, robust models, or estimand-focused analyses may be preferable; Mann-Whitney does not automatically test means or medians. |
| For a simple linear relationship, `r = 0.800` implies `r² = 0.640`, or 64% of sample variance explained by the fitted relationship. | Statistical calculation | PREP export, Question 16; Schober P, Boer C, Schwarte LA. *Anesth Analg*. 2018;126:1763-1768. PMID [29481436](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29481436/), DOI [10.1213/ANE.0000000000002864](https://doi.org/10.1213/ANE.0000000000002864) | Squaring 0.800 gives 0.640. Schober et al describe correlation and coefficient-of-determination interpretation. | Verified with language correction | The source uses causal-sounding “attributed” language. The deck instead says statistically explained in the fitted sample relationship and explicitly rejects causal attribution. The source population is adult; only the calculation is generalized to pediatrics. |
| A run chart is the listed tool that displays performance in time order and can reveal a sustained post-change shift. | Quality-improvement method | PREP export, Question 44; Perla RJ, Provost LP, Murray SK. *BMJ Qual Saf*. 2011;20:46-51. DOI [10.1136/bmjqs.2009.037895](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs.2009.037895); IHI, [Run Chart Tool](https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/run-chart-tool) | Perla et al describe run charts as time-ordered data displays and give nonrandom-pattern rules. The PREP item keys run chart. | Verified | Published conventions differ on the exact shift threshold; the deck labels six or more as a common rule rather than a universal law. |
